Debris-Flow Model Evaluation

After a wildfire, a heavy burst of rain can send mud and rock down the burned slopes. We tested how well a published U.S. Geological Survey formula, and several machine-learning models built on NASA satellite data, predict whether that happens. This page shows what worked, what did not, and how sure we are.

The short version

The USGS formula ranks risk well

Across 20 past fires, it put debris-flow storms above quiet ones about 88 times out of 100 (AUC 0.88).

But its raw percentages run too high

When it said 90% or more, debris flows happened about 40% of the time. Rescaling the numbers on past data fixed this.

More machine learning did not help

None of the ML models beat the rescaled formula. Satellite rain was far less useful than rain gauges.

How we tested

We used the USGS inventory of post-fire storms: 7,639 observations from 77 fires (fire years 2000 to 2024, storms from July 2000 to September 2025), each marked as debris flow or no debris flow. The model comparison below uses the 20 fires that also had NASA satellite coverage.

Every score comes from leave-one-fire-out testing. A model is built from all fires except one, then graded on the fire it never saw. This repeats for each fire. It is the closest we can get to asking how the model would do on the next fire.

The USGS formula (called M1) combines how steeply and severely a basin burned, how erodible its soil is, and how hard the rain fell. Recalibrated M1 is the same formula with its output rescaled to match what actually happened in the inventory.

Can you trust the percentages?

The raw formula overstates risk. After rescaling, a forecast of 30% really did come out near 30%.

Can satellite rain replace rain gauges?

Not yet. Driving the same formula with NASA IMERG satellite rain instead of gauge rain dropped its ranking skill from about 0.87 to about 0.55–0.65.

AUC measures ranking: 1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is a coin flip. Debris flows depend on rain bursts lasting minutes. IMERG averages over roughly 10 km, so it smooths those bursts away.

Does satellite data add anything?

Soil moisture (SMAP) mattered most inside the flexible ML model, but that model still scored worse than the rescaled formula.

We removed one group of inputs at a time from the full ML model and measured how much its error grew. Longer bars mean the model leaned on that group more.

Removing season slightly improved the model, which suggests it was adding noise. Adding SMAP to the rescaled formula made no clear difference (change +0.002, range −0.002 to +0.007).

What this does not show

  • No test on Eaton or Palisades. The rescaled model on our Eaton and Palisades maps is experimental. It has not been checked against what happened in those fires.
  • The inventory is lopsided. Some basins appear many times across storms, and a few big fires dominate. Scores for any single fire can differ from the pooled result.
  • Rescaling is fitted to past fires. Those fires are mostly in the western U.S. A California-only fit gives somewhat different numbers, so the percentages carry real uncertainty.
  • Rain is the key input. The formula needs rainfall intensity over 15, 30 or 60 minutes. Until satellite rain captures short bursts, the tool is best used with radar or gauge forecasts.
  • Rescaled percentages look low. Most basins come out under 20% for typical storms. That reflects how rarely debris flows occur, and it does not mean they are safe.

Plain-language glossary

Brier score
The average squared miss between a forecast percentage and what happened (1 for a debris flow, 0 for none). Lower is better. Compare it to the score from always guessing the average.
AUC
Pick one storm that caused a debris flow and one that did not. AUC is how often the model gave the first a higher risk. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect.
Calibration
Whether forecasts match reality. Among storms forecast at 30%, about 3 in 10 should produce debris flows.
Leave-one-fire-out
Test each fire using a model that never saw it, so the score is not flattered by memorizing.
Bootstrap
Re-running the comparison on many reshuffled sets of fires to see how much the result depends on which fires were included.

Data and sources

USGS Post-Fire Debris-Flow Inventory (2017 Staley et al. M1 model) · NASA GPM IMERG V07 rainfall · NASA SMAP L4 soil moisture · NASA HLS v2.0 imagery · NASADEM elevation · SSURGO soils · NOAA RONI index. Results are from our own analysis of these data and have not been peer reviewed.

Validation with actual fires

Here each fire is a separate test. For every fire, the models were built from all the other fires, then graded on how well they matched what really happened on the fire they never saw.

This tab uses all 77 fires in the USGS inventory and the 30-minute rain version of each model. “Rescaled” is published M1 with its output rescaled to past outcomes. “ML” is the gradient-boosting model used on the Eaton and Palisades maps (burn extent, burn severity, soil and gauge rain intensity). Neither Eaton nor Palisades is in the inventory, so they are not tested here.

Published M1 ran too high on most fires

On 22 of 28 fires with at least 30 storms, the rescaled or ML model had lower error than published M1.

Except where debris flows were widespread

Published M1 did best on fires where most storms did produce debris flows, such as Grand Prix–Old (77%), Little Bear (64%) and Cameron Peak (67%). Rescaling pulls numbers down, which helps on most fires and hurts on these.

Los Angeles-area fires disagree

Thomas (2017) and Fish (2016) favored the rescaled models. Station (2009) favored published M1. That is why Eaton and Palisades still need a real test.

How much to trust the percentages

Use the ranking with more confidence than the percentage. On a new fire, the share of storms with debris flows was often two to four times higher or lower than the model’s average forecast.

For each of the 25 fires with at least 30 storms and at least one debris flow, we compared the model’s average forecast with what happened. The table shows how often it landed close.

What that means for a 20% forecast

  • About 4 in 10 fires ended up between 10% and 40% (within 2×).
  • About 7 in 10 fires ended up between 7% and 60% (within 3×).
  • About 8 in 10 fires ended up between 5% and 80% (within 4×).

Ranking holds up better. The rescaled model had lower error than the “always guess” baseline on 23 of 28 fires (published M1: 7 of 28). Among the eight fires first checked for ranking skill (Thomas, Station, Fish, Dolan, Dixie, Grand Prix–Old, Butte and Ferguson), AUC was 0.80 to 0.93 on six, and lower on Dolan (0.60 to 0.67) and Grand Prix–Old (about 0.69).

These are fire-wide averages. Individual catchments within a fire vary more. With 25 fires, the percentages above are rough guides, not formal confidence intervals.

What this does not show

  • Eaton and Palisades are untested. They are not in the inventory, and no per-catchment record of what happened is public. Satellite checks (OPERA radar and disturbance maps, HLS change) were too weak to count as a test.
  • Published M1 has a head start on some fires. Station (2009) and Grand Prix–Old (2003) belong to the 2003–2009 California group the published formula was built from, so its good scores there are probably flattered.
  • One fire is a small test. Scores swing a lot from fire to fire, especially for fires with few storms or very few debris flows.
  • The ML model’s edge is small. Over the whole inventory it scores slightly better than rescaled M1, but the bootstrap range includes no improvement at 30 minutes. It also differs from the satellite-rain ML models on the Overall results tab.
  • 30-minute rain only. The 15- and 60-minute versions were not broken out by fire.
